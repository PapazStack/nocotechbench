**Short answer: a prebuilt can be a really good deal right now.** But only if the parts that aren't on the box hold up. The big-name CPU and graphics card don't tell the whole story. Check the RAM, the exact GPU, the power supply, and the motherboard before you pay.

I build and fix PCs around Fort Collins, Loveland, Greeley, and Windsor. "Is this prebuilt a good deal?" is one of the questions I get most. Here's how I check one.

## Why prebuilts make more sense this year

For years, the rule was simple. Build it yourself and save money. That rule doesn't always hold in 2026.

Memory is the big reason. [Newegg's November 2 write-up](https://www.newegg.com/insider/prebuilt-vs-diy-gaming-pc-late-2026) says RAM supply has been tight all year, and DDR5 prices went up with it. It also says SK Hynix expects memory shortages to last until 2030. Big PC builders often bought RAM and SSDs in bulk before prices jumped. So some prebuilts now cost about the same as the parts alone, or even less.

[How-To Geek](https://www.howtogeek.com/dont-fall-for-these-common-traps-when-buying-a-prebuilt-pc/) says the same thing, and it gives a good tip. Price the same parts on PCPartPicker. A prebuilt usually costs a bit more for assembly and testing. But it shouldn't cost a lot more than the parts.

So don't skip a prebuilt just because "building is always cheaper." Do the math first.

## 1. Check if the RAM is one stick or two

A listing that says "16GB RAM" can mean one 16GB stick or two 8GB sticks. That matters. Two sticks run in dual channel. Newegg points out that one stick cuts your memory bandwidth in half.

Look for "2x8GB" or "2x16GB" in the spec sheet. One stick isn't a deal breaker. Just plan to buy a matching stick, and add that to the price.

Already bought it? Open Task Manager. Go to Performance, then Memory. Look at "Slots used." If it says 1 of 4, you've got one stick.

## 2. Check the exact graphics card

Some GPU names cover more than one card. The RTX 5060 Ti comes with 8GB or 16GB of video memory, [per NVIDIA's spec page](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/geforce/graphics-cards/50-series/rtx-5060-family/). The listing title might not tell you which one you're getting.

Find the GPU memory size in the full spec sheet. For big open-world games, Newegg leans toward the 16GB version. I'd do the same if the price gap is small.

Also watch for vague lines like "8GB graphics card" with no model name. If the seller won't name the card, I'd move on.

## 3. Check the power supply

The power supply is where cheap prebuilts often cut corners. How-To Geek says some builders use no-name units that barely cover what the PC needs. That can cause crashes and shutdowns while you game.

A good listing names the brand, the wattage, and the efficiency rating, like 80 Plus Bronze or Gold. Just "650W" with nothing else is a reason to ask questions.

Think about later upgrades too. A power supply with no extra room means you'll need a new one before a bigger graphics card goes in. For example, NVIDIA lists 600W of system power for the RTX 5060 Ti.

## 4. Check the motherboard and case

Lots of listings skip the motherboard. But it decides how much RAM you can add, how many SSDs fit, and which CPU upgrades you can do later.

Some brands use custom boards, power supplies, or cases that don't follow normal ATX sizes. How-To Geek notes that this can make upgrades and out-of-warranty repairs harder. If you want to upgrade later, look for standard parts and free RAM and M.2 slots.

## 5. Check the cooling

A strong CPU with a tiny cooler can run hot and slow down. Look for the exact cooler model in the spec sheet. And more fans doesn't always mean better airflow.

Don't buy on looks either. RGB lights and a "gamer" case tell you nothing about the parts inside.

## What to do in the first hour

If you buy one, Newegg has a short checklist that I agree with:

1. Check "Slots used" and the memory speed in Task Manager.
2. Turn on XMP or EXPO in the BIOS if the RAM runs below its rated speed.
3. Get the newest graphics driver from NVIDIA or AMD.
4. Remove the trial apps you don't want.
5. Play a demanding game for about 20 minutes and watch your temps.

Do all of this inside the return window. A problem is much easier to fix then.

## My take

A prebuilt can be the smart buy this year. Just treat the CPU and GPU as the start of your check, not the end. Do the RAM, GPU memory, power supply, and motherboard all check out? Is the price close to the parts total? Then it's probably a good deal.
