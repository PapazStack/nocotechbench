**Yep, DDR3 RAM can still be worth buying if you're keeping an old PC.** I'd just check that it actually needs more memory first. For a new gaming build, I'd put that money toward a newer setup instead.

I work on PCs in Fort Collins, Loveland, Greeley, and Windsor, and I get this question a lot. My first question back is usually, "What do you want the computer to do?"

## So, what is DDR3?

DDR3 is an older generation of RAM. Think of RAM as the space your apps use to hold data while they're running. Your SSD or hard drive keeps your files for the long term.

[Kingston explains RAM and storage](https://www.kingston.com/en/blog/gaming/the-ultimate-ram-guide-for-gamers) if you want a closer look. More RAM gives you room to keep more apps or files open at once.

You'll find DDR3 in older desktops and laptops. For example, [Intel's Core i5-4590](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/products/sku/80815/intel-core-i54590-processor-6m-cache-up-to-3-70-ghz/specifications.html) supports DDR3 and DDR3L. But don't take that as a match for every older Intel PC.

Your processor and motherboard decide which memory you can use. For comparison, [AMD's AM4 platform uses DDR4, while AM5 uses DDR5](https://www.amd.com/en/products/processors/chipsets.html).

**DDR3, DDR4, and DDR5 aren't interchangeable.** Their slots and notches differ, so you can't just swap between generations. [Kingston's DDR4 guide](https://www.kingston.com/en/memory/ddr4-overview) explains why DDR3 and DDR4 don't fit each other's boards. Its RAM guide covers DDR5's different fit, too.

If a stick doesn't fit, don't force it. You'll need a board that supports that memory generation.

## When I'd buy it

If your PC still does what you need, I wouldn't write it off just because it uses DDR3. Maybe you want to keep using it for email, documents, and browsing.

I'd check that the rest of the PC still meets your needs and its software still gets security updates. Then I'd make sure the extra memory stays within the system's limits.

If your usual apps use most of your RAM, a small upgrade may make sense. Have a look while the PC is actually slowing down. [Microsoft's performance guide](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/experience/performance-optimization/tips-to-improve-pc-performance-in-windows) explains how Task Manager shows CPU, memory, and disk use.

Seeing lots of memory in use doesn't mean your RAM is broken. It's something to check, not a reason to order replacement sticks right away.

## When I'd save the money

I wouldn't start a new gaming build with DDR3 today. I'd compare the whole cost with a newer processor, motherboard, and RAM setup before buying anything.

The same goes for a used PC. Ask for the exact CPU and motherboard models, not just how much RAM it has. Then check what your games need.

I wouldn't take "gaming PC" in a listing as proof that it'll do what you want. And I wouldn't promise extra frames just because you add RAM.

If the computer keeps acting up, let's figure out why before throwing parts at it. Microsoft also lists low disk space and too many startup apps as possible causes of slow performance.

## Before you order anything

Grab the motherboard manual before you start shopping. It can save you a lot of guessing.

You'll also see something called a QVL, or Qualified Vendor List. That's the list of memory the maker has tested. [ASUS shows how to search its QVL](https://www.asus.com/support/faq/1043883/) for an exact memory model. Its [installation guide](https://rog.asus.com/support/faq/1047257/) also points you to the slot order in the manual.

Here's what I'd check:

1. Get the exact model name for your PC or motherboard.
2. Pull up its memory support page on the maker's website.
3. Check the manual for memory type, speed, and how much RAM it supports.
4. Look for the exact RAM part number on the QVL.
5. Check the system specs for the voltage it needs.
6. Make sure you're buying the right module size.

If you're still unsure, ask the PC or board maker before spending anything.

## Watch for DDR3L and laptop sizes

This is the bit I'd double-check, especially with a laptop.

Standard DDR3 uses 1.5 V. DDR3L supports the lower voltage of 1.35 V. [Dell explains that DDR3L laptop modules also support 1.5 V](https://www.dell.com/support/kbdoc/en-us/000132438/what-is-ddr3l-memory).

If your laptop calls for DDR3L, stick with what its specs say. Standard DDR3 might fit but keep the laptop from starting. The right notch doesn't tell you the whole story.

Desktops usually take full-size DIMMs, while laptops often take smaller SO-DIMMs. Those sizes don't fit the same slots. [Kingston covers both formats](https://www.kingston.com/en/blog/gaming/choosing-overclock-ram), including small PCs that also use SO-DIMMs. Some laptops have memory fixed to the board, so there's no slot to upgrade.

That's really my take: DDR3 can be a sensible fix for a PC you're happy to keep. I just wouldn't make it the starting point for a new gaming build.
